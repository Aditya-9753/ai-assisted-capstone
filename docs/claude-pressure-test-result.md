Portfolio Sitemap Review
1. Does every page earn its place?

Yes — this is about as lean as a professional portfolio gets, and that's a strength, not a weakness. Each page has a distinct job:

Home/Hero — first impression, filters/qualifies the visitor in 5 seconds
Work/Case Studies — the actual evidence
About — credibility and context for the evidence
Contact — conversion point

Nothing here is decorative. I'd flag it if you'd included a blog, a "skills" page with progress bars, a testimonials page, or a "services" page — those are the usual bloat for a solo dev portfolio, and you've correctly avoided them. Don't add them unless a specific piece of evidence demands it.

2. Does the sitemap clearly support the proof statement?

Structurally yes, but the proof statement lives or dies entirely on what's inside Work/Case Studies — the sitemap itself can't prove anything, only point to the page that must.

Your proof statement makes three specific claims: reliable applications, REST APIs/backend systems, and solving real business problems. A sitemap with one generic "Work" page risks becoming a portfolio-shaped container with nothing calibrated inside it. The structure is right; the risk is in execution, not architecture.

3. Does the sitemap guide visitors toward the primary action?

Partially. Right now Contact is a destination, not a target visitors get pulled toward. A recruiter who lands on Home, glances at Work, and skims About should have Contact reinforced along the way, not just available as a final nav item.

4. Is anything unnecessary or missing?

Unnecessary: Nothing to cut. Four pages for this proof statement is correct scope.

Missing (not new pages — reinforcement within existing pages):

No repeated call-to-action. If Contact is only in the nav, you're relying on visitors to self-initiate the action at the end of their visit, after attention has already decayed.
Case Studies risks being one flat page instead of individual case study entries — with one Python developer, "case studies" (plural) implies multiple, each ideally its own detailed unit showing problem → approach → outcome. That structure directly serves the "real-world business problems" half of your proof statement — vague summaries don't.
5. Specific recommendation

Add a secondary CTA at the end of the Work/Case Studies page and at the end of each individual case study, not just in Home's hero and the nav bar.

Why: the primary action is "contact me for a development opportunity." The Work page is where a hiring manager forms their opinion of your ability — that's the exact moment they decide whether to reach out, not five minutes later after scrolling to About. If the CTA only lives in the nav, you're asking them to remember to act instead of prompting them at peak interest. Something as simple as "Interested in working together? [Contact]" after each case study closes that gap without adding a page or diluting the sitemap.

One thing I can't assess without more input: whether "Work/Case Studies" is currently planned as one page or a set of individual case study pages (Work index → individual case study detail pages). That distinction matters a lot for how well you can prove the "reliable, scalable, real-world" claims. Which is it?
